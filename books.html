Zero to One

This book focuses on the important of building new markets. Different to what I am focusing on at the moment. It talks about 7 important 'greens' (teams, defensibility, engineering (and its relation to competition), product deliverability, timing, and monopoly). Other ideas:

  • You need to do inherently new stuff to win (e.g. Apple iPhone vs. other companies just iterating at the time).
  • The network effect is a powerful thing.
  • Follow the power law. Only a small set of outcomes are worth more than all other outcomes combined. Just as a lot of successful portfolios have one big win that outweighs all losses.
  • The value need to be captured not just created.
  • Man and machine need to each play the role they are good at. (Data crunching vs. thinking)
  • All good companies have secrets they rely on. Stuff that if it got out, would cause a big issue for company.
  • Be the last mover (to do it well). First mover not actually a big advantage.
  • Selling $1k product very hard at scale!!!
  • Choose cofounders like marriage

Ideas I'll actually use:

  1. The best startups create a monopoly by genuinely creating something new. Scaling vertically 0 -> 1 oppose to horizontally 1 -> n
  2. Aim to have all 7: 1. Engineering (10x > competition), 2. Defensibility (Can China clone), 3. Timing, 4. Monopoly, 5. People, 6. Distribution, 7. Secret
  3. Your startup must have a secret. Secret often born from: What is something you STRONGLY believe, that goes against the norm?
  4. Design > luck. Intelligent design beats evolution. Motorola was iterating on what was working for cellphone users, Apple came out with iPhone.
  5. Power law. Follow money. Everyone is an investor, whether you are a employee or a founder.
  6. Network effects can and should be abused.
  7. Creating value isn't enough, you need to capture it. Airlines do this poorly, large cost but only make small margin on each person. Rely on extreme volume.
  8. Too hard to sell $1k product at scale. Either $10 virally, or $10k+, paying people to close.
  9. Man's relationship to machine is important. Don't design to replace a human, design to make their work more efficient. E.g. LinkedIn didn't replace recruiters, it just assisted them.

The Mom Test

Thesis in short:

  • A false positive from a potential end user is far worse than no data at all. Talk about their lives, you don't want them to even know you are trying to sell a product.

Ideas I'll actually use:

  1. Compliments are bad (or at least should be ignored.)
  2. No need for setting up business meetings, just run into them, be involved in their world.
  3. The conversation should not feel like you are trying to sell a product. Phrases:
    • "Why do you bother to X" (X = slow work around to a problem)
    • "What are the implications for that?" (is this REALLY a problem?)
    • "Talk me through the last time it happened"
    • "How are you dealing with this now?"
    • "Who else should I talk to?"
  4. Never to into pitch mode
  5. Never expose your ego
  6. Start the conversations very broad, let the end user zoom in. Let them tell you what part of the problem really burns them.
  7. Prepare a list of questions for each end user you are discussing with

Obviously Awesome

Thesis in short:

  • Market positioning is more important than product quality in regards to selling products. A professional violinist may get $38 in a subway and $10,000 in a concert hall.

Ideas I'll actually use:

  1. The most valuable yet hardest thing to do is building your own category; Thus you completely own market positioning.
  2. Customers will likely default to a spreadsheet, paper and pen, hiring someone or doing nothing if they don't buy your product, not a competitor.
  3. Disregard your original assumptions for market positioning. Always be open to change in this regard. Ask other members of the team.
  4. Good ways to capture customers attention is to work within a space that is known and customers understand, but bring something that differentiates.
  5. If you don't position correctly, the customer will auto-position you, 99% of the time in a box you don't want to be in.
  6. Talk to a lot of people to understand their market position.

Gap Selling

Thesis in one sentence:

  • To sell a something you must identify a clear gap between their current state and where they need to be. Sales isn't about selling its about convincing them to move from where they are to where they need to be.

Ideas I'll actually use:

  1. Never demo before discovery
  2. Understand what the buying process is
    • Understand the clients emotional position on the gap
  3. The gap needs to be a part of leadership. It needs to be embedded in the team.
  4. Use command statements during discovery
    • "Walk me through X", "Help me understand why you do Y."
  5. Use affirming questions during demo:
    • "Can you see how this will improve X?"
  6. A new buyer (could be same company, literally any different individual) should have a new discovery conversation.
  7. A client will only meet with you if it is their best interest to do so...
  8. Diagnose the current state OBSESSIVELY, find the intrinsic motivation and quantify the gap.

The Ideal Team Player

Thesis in short:

  • Humble: Not concerned with getting credit; Elevating the team.
  • Hungry: Always looking to fill in the gaps and learn. Self-motivated.
  • Smart: You are group-smart. Your goal is to understand how to interact with different people. Do you understand how what you just said made the other person feel?

Ideas I'll actually use:

  • While a person can never be 100% in all categories, there is an innately unfair (ask Kahu how he knows), line in the sand which should be reached for all three stats.
  • These rules will cause issues. These rules only pay off when you step back and look at the business as a whole.
  • With a good enough manager. Anyone can become a 'good' (according to these rules) team member.

No Pit Stops

Thesis in short:

  • Stick with stuff. A team in business must have someone who is very good at sales; lawyers are good too. Everyday (boring) products have mad people behind them, who turned them from something in a garage into something multi-national. Don't take your health for granted.

Ideas I will actually use:

  • Acquisitions are not just done to remove competitors but can be done to turn the acquirer around.
  • Always ask. Think cashing a $26 million check at 5:02 on a Friday.
  • Biggest takeaway: The 1->n philosophy works well when you are looking at capturing more value (Rid the leaky bucket!) This is seen when Grant talks about changing how rental agreements contracts were written at blue star; and not letting car insurers take their business from turners.

A Philosophy of Software Design

Ideas I'll actually use:

  1. Depth > Length. Depth = What does this module do for me?/ What do I need to know to use it?
  2. Good design is done by emphasizing what you do need, and minimizing what you don't.
  3. Not from this book, just idea: When designing modular code, adapters/ seams should be clear. Seam = where module meets something it needs to be plumbed into (best space for testing). Adapter = required to do conversion at seam.
  4. Interfaces are contracts. E.g. I give you X, you give me Y.
  5. Objects store data and func's related, required to do something.
  6. Classes are blueprints for objects.
  7. Define errors out of existence. Just: else -> Fix
  8. Focus on making reading easy, not writing (make interfaces simple first, then focus on their implementation)
  9. Pull down complexity

Disciplined Entrepreneurship

You must be passionate, and understand the space. A founding team should have someone technologically savvy, someone good at marketing/ sales and someone who can manage community and design.

Group 1. (Who is the customer?)

  • Market segmentation: Brainstorm side list of possible market opportunities. Done before beachhead selection
  • Select a beachhead market: Choosing a tiny, very specific market to start off and dominate to validate ideas
  • Build an end user profile: Priorities, watering holes, cares about X over Y...
  • Calculate TAM (for beachhead): Used to understand if good enough opportunity
  • Profile and persona for beachhead market: What is their name, age, habits, favourite food, etc. Actual person.
  • Identify your next 10 customers: Who else matches your end user profile to validate your assumptions hold across multiple customers.

Group 2. (What can you do for your customer?)

  • Full life cycle use case: How does the customer discover acquire uses and gets value from your product in e2e lifecycle.
  • High level production specification: Outline what benefits this product brings to customers
  • Quantify the value proposition: Explain numerically how the customer will benefit from this
  • Define your core: Secret sauce/ competitive moat. E.g. Network effect; NOT a business model.
  • Competitive Position: Top 2 priorities from top customer from beach head market plotted against one another

Group 3. (How does your customer acquire your product?)

  • Decision making unit (DMU): What is required for a customer to say yes to something. E.g. Primary economic buyer, influencers, champion, (someone who wants your product bought, influencers, veto power, Purchasing department.
  • Map the process to acquire a paying customer: ?
  • Map the sales process to acquire a customer: How much money and time does it take to acquire a customer. Can this be shortened?

Group 4. (How do you make money off this product?)

  • Design a business model: Monthly payment, one time payment, ads, etc
  • Set your pricing framework: Calculate based on TLV and COCA from client. Also how much value the product adds.
  • Calculate LTV of customer: Life time value of a customer is based of recurring payments, etc
  • Calculate COCA: Cost of customer acquisition is calculated top down (total sales + market spend) / new customers

Group 5. (How do you design and build your product?)

  • Identify key assumptions: Profile and persona want to buy goods from X country
  • Test key assumptions: Cheapest way to test all assumptions
  • Define MVBP: How can we best include all assumptions to make testing efficient. Customer gets value, customer pays, and it starts feedback loop (literal feedback)
  • Show that dogs will eat dog food: Even though all the signals say that the profile and persona want to buy X goods, do they actually?

Group 6. (How do you scale your business?)

  • Develop a product (not just business) plan: Product evolution and market expansion past MVBP.
  • Calculate TAM for follow on markets: How much can your business expand for the next 10 customers (past beachhead market)
