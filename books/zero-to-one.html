Zero to One

This book focuses on the important of building new markets. Different to what I am focusing on at the moment. It talks about 7 important 'greens' (teams, defensibility, engineering (and its relation to competition), product deliverability, timing, and monopoly). Other ideas:

  • You need to do inherently new stuff to win (e.g. Apple iPhone vs. other companies just iterating at the time).
  • The network effect is a powerful thing.
  • Follow the power law. Only a small set of outcomes are worth more than all other outcomes combined. Just as a lot of successful portfolios have one big win that outweighs all losses.
  • The value need to be captured not just created.
  • Man and machine need to each play the role they are good at. (Data crunching vs. thinking)
  • All good companies have secrets they rely on. Stuff that if it got out, would cause a big issue for company.
  • Be the last mover (to do it well). First mover not actually a big advantage.
  • Selling $1k product very hard at scale!!!
  • Choose cofounders like marriage

Ideas I'll actually use:

  1. The best startups create a monopoly by genuinely creating something new. Scaling vertically 0 -> 1 oppose to horizontally 1 -> n
  2. Aim to have all 7: 1. Engineering (10x > competition), 2. Defensibility (Can China clone), 3. Timing, 4. Monopoly, 5. People, 6. Distribution, 7. Secret
  3. Your startup must have a secret. Secret often born from: What is something you STRONGLY believe, that goes against the norm?
  4. Design > luck. Intelligent design beats evolution. Motorola was iterating on what was working for cellphone users, Apple came out with iPhone.
  5. Power law. Follow money. Everyone is an investor, whether you are a employee or a founder.
  6. Network effects can and should be abused.
  7. Creating value isn't enough, you need to capture it. Airlines do this poorly, large cost but only make small margin on each person. Rely on extreme volume.
  8. Too hard to sell $1k product at scale. Either $10 virally, or $10k+, paying people to close.
  9. Man's relationship to machine is important. Don't design to replace a human, design to make their work more efficient. E.g. LinkedIn didn't replace recruiters, it just assisted them.
